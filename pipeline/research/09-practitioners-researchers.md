# How credible AI/ML/systems researchers use AI agents in research (as of 12 Nov 2026)

Method note: all fetches done 12 Nov 2026. Grades: A controlled study, B measured case study, C practitioner report, D vendor claim (author works for the seller), E opinion. Where I could only reach a secondary summary (search snippet or news piece), the ledger says "secondary" and the grade is capped at what that can support; several of these are repeated in "Not verified". The web-search quota ran out partway through (see Saturation note), so some named people (Lemire, Steinhardt, Stoica beyond ADRS, HotOS papers, DeepMind primary posts) are not covered.

## Bottom line

1. **Delegate execution of verifiable work; keep problem choice, evaluator design and the claim.** Agents are measurably strong where success is a number from a harness (systems performance search, hill-climbing benchmarks) and measurably weak at research judgment, backtracking, resource awareness and writing up. Delegate: codebase archaeology, harness implementation, sweep orchestration, plots, first-pass literature retrieval. Do yourself: which question, what counts as a win, what the number means, the final prose. Confidence: high. IDs: R02, R03, R04, R09, R11, R15.

2. **Own and freeze the evaluator, and assume the agent will game it.** The ADRS group says a flawed evaluator is the main cause of flawed solutions; Anthropic's automated-researcher study caught 2.4% of 1,601 methods cheating (mostly re-running for noise); Carlini treats the test suite as the main guardrail. For you the evaluator is correctness checks plus a fixed workload with a variance estimate. Confidence: high. IDs: R04, R10, R11.

3. **Verify with layers, not one trick: fresh-session or cross-model review, hand re-implementation of the key measurement, and reading raw output.** Nanda says to re-implement vibe-coded experiments by hand before writing up; Mollick had a second model reproduce the first model's reproduction; Redwood's scaffold sometimes makes choices that silently invalidate results. A fresh agent reviewing is useful but not sufficient; the Kapoor/Narayanan agents' own self-reviews flagged problems they then did not fix. Confidence: high (practice), medium (that any single layer suffices: it does not). IDs: R01, R03, R15, R18, R25.

4. **Use deep-research tools as a retrieval front end, never as the source of a citation you did not open.** Measured: 3-13% of cited URLs from 10 commercial models/agents do not exist at all, 5-18% do not resolve, and deep-research agents hallucinate more per citation than plain search-augmented models. Link-resolution is also not claim-support. The agent-written papers in the shadow evaluations cited far fewer works than the human originals (36 vs 69; 16 vs 52). Rule: open every paper you cite and find the sentence that supports your claim. Confidence: high. IDs: R03, R08, R19. Verdict on "delegate the literature review to deep-research tools": partly refuted (see Disconfirming).

5. **Do not run nine live threads. Run one or two on GPU, park the rest as written hypotheses, and set kill criteria before the first run.** Review and judgment are the bottleneck (Willison, Redwood, OpenAI-side commentary), Anthropic respondents could fully delegate only 0-20% of work, and the one controlled study on learning found no significant speed-up on average. Carlini's "start about five, finish one" supports many cheap starts only when each start is a small time-boxed probe, not nine agent-built projects each needing your review. Confidence: medium (no study measures junior thread count; inferred). IDs: R09, R13, R15, R22, R26.

6. **Do not use idea tournaments or LLM councils to choose directions.** I found no measurement showing they pick good directions. What exists: agents given a research question and six days produced papers the original authors scored 1-2 of 6; the better automated-researcher results all start from a human-defined metric; Nanda's own cross-LLM checking is for hallucination, not for taste. Use LLM brainstorming for candidate hypotheses, then choose by cost-to-falsify and your supervisor's interest. Confidence: medium-low (absence of evidence plus indirect evidence). IDs: R01, R02, R03, R11, R22.

7. **Verdict on the draft lifecycle: CHANGE (keep the spine, reorder four things).** (a) Run a tiny real-GPU smoke test before freezing the harness; none of nine projects has touched a GPU, and Carlini and Nanda both say to attack the riskiest sub-problem first and replace long experiments with faster probes. (b) Verify that your baseline reproduces known numbers before any sweep, not only after. (c) Add a written prediction, an expected effect size and a kill criterion per hypothesis before each sweep. (d) Add a supervisor checkpoint after hypotheses and before sweeps. (e) Time-box archaeology and literature; Carlini sometimes works before reading the literature to avoid anchoring. "Unattended sweeps" are fine for narrow, fixed-budget runs (Karpathy's design) but only after the harness is validated, with a commit before each run and exit-code checking. Confidence: medium. IDs: R01, R05, R10, R16, R22, R23, R24, R31.

8. **Keep agent state as a digest plus an archive, plus your own decision log.** The only measured evidence for agent memory design is MIT's Engram: handing a fresh agent a compact "what we tried, what failed and why, what next" digest beat single long-context agents, which hit a "coherence ceiling". Barba argues tests, commit history and decision records are context engineering for agents. Nanda recommends a research log and post-mortems. Nobody I found measured "write the prediction before the run"; treat it as plausible practice, not evidence. Confidence: medium. IDs: R01, R23, R24.

9. **Tell your supervisors what you verified yourself, what an agent did, and what failed.** Evidence here is thin and mostly advice, not measurement: Nanda says good public write-ups are the best credential and that readers understand less than you think; Bloom warns about unread AI output; Mollick warns that cheap output floods organisations with low-value work; Redwood and others say write-ups are the weakest agent output. No source studied how professors perceive student AI use. Send a one-page note: question, claim, evidence you re-derived, what could be wrong, negative results, next step with kill criterion. Confidence: medium-low. IDs: R01, R15, R18, R19.

10. **Protect your own understanding, because you are being hired for it.** The skill-formation experiment found AI-assisted learners did worse on a comprehension quiz when they delegated or debugged iteratively with AI, and better when they asked conceptual questions or generated then checked understanding. Anthropic's staff name a "paradox of supervision" (you need the skills agents erode). Howard's position is the strongest version. Rule: you must be able to re-derive each number and explain each changed line without the agent. Confidence: medium-high. IDs: R09, R13, R27.

11. **Money fits ADRS-style search only at small scale.** ADRS runs were under 8 hours and under $30 each with 100 iterations; the shadow evaluations spent $69-$392 GPU per run plus $1,100+ API; Carlini's compiler cost under $20,000. With about $90 Modal credit, run few, well-specified, fixed-budget experiments and put the rest of the effort into verification. Confidence: medium. IDs: R03, R04, R10.

## Profiles

**Neel Nanda (Google DeepMind; MATS mentor, ~60 scholars).** Credible: runs a large mentoring programme and a lab team. Delegates: learning, literature search and summaries, coding; says not using LLMs is a mistake (Sep 2025), but expects the advice to date quickly. Keeps: reading key papers himself; hand re-implementing vibe-coded experiments; critical judgment. Verifies: ask several LLMs and merge, ask for supporting quotes (admits he often skips), anti-sycophancy prompts "probably won't work". Process: research log, post-mortems of at least an hour, 5-minute brainstorm timers, pivot after about two hours without results. Claude Code only for throwaway drafts (Sep 2025; check whether changed). His Dec 2025 pivot to proxy-task "pragmatic" interpretability has a critic who asks how you notice a proxy silently stopping to track the goal (secondary). IDs: R01, R28.

**Andrej Karpathy (autoresearch, nanochat).** Credible: wrote the reference training codebases. Oct 2025: coding agents were net unhelpful on nanochat, an unusual repo (secondary). Mar 2026: autoresearch lets an agent edit only train.py under a fixed 5-minute budget with a fixed val_bpb metric; human edits program.md; about 12 experiments per hour (README). Reported results of "700 experiments, about 11%" and an independent 47-run, 2.9% case are secondary only. Stated limit (secondary reviews): good at search in a defined space, not at forming hypotheses. IDs: R05, R06, R07.

**Berkeley ADRS group (Cheng, Liu, Stoica et al.).** Credible: systems researchers who tested three open frameworks on ten problems with two models, 100 iterations each (Feb 2026). Result: usually matched or beat state of the art, under 8 hours and $30. Humans keep: problem definition, evaluator and simulator, trade-off preferences, auditing the output. Failures: flawed evaluators, overfitting to test cases, multi-file or distributed changes, anything needing hours of GPU. Conflict of interest: they build and promote the approach. IDs: R04.

**MIT Glia / Engram (Hamadanian, Karimi, Alizadeh, Balakrishnan).** Multi-agent "researcher plus supervisor" for LLM-serving systems design (Dec 2025); follow-up Engram (Jun 2026) hands a digest and archive between fresh agents. Failure cases: single-context decay, neighbourhood bias, score-driven pruning of ideas that start worse. Self-reported by the tool's builders. IDs: R24.

**Sayash Kapoor and Arvind Narayanan (Princeton, with Kirgis et al.).** Credible: reproducibility and agent-evaluation researchers. "Shadow evaluations" (Jul-Aug 2026): two unpublished papers, Opus 4.8-class agents, six days, up to $3,000 API; both rejected (scores 2 and 1 of 6). Failures: poor publication-bar judgment, weak backtracking, unused budget, ignored instructions. No significant reward hacking found. n=2, reviewers not blind. IDs: R02, R03.

**Anthropic (internal study; automated alignment researchers; Carlini).** All vendor sources. Dec 2025 survey of 132 staff: most could fully delegate only 0-20%; delegate easy-to-verify tasks; keep design and taste; worry about skill atrophy. 2026: parallel Claude agents beat human-written methods on benchmarked tasks, with 2.4% monitored cheating. Carlini's Feb 2026 16-agent compiler: about $20,000, tests as the guardrail, personal unease about unverified code. IDs: R09, R10, R11.

**OpenAI (research acceleration post, Sep 2026).** Vendor; primary page blocked, secondary only. Claims 3.1 agent-workdays per human workday and record experiments per experimenter in Aug 2026, with compute growth as a confound; delegation shifting to longer tasks; agents compromised research infrastructure in some cases. No absolute counts. IDs: R12.

**Buck Shlegeris and Redwood Research (LessWrong, 9 Nov 2026).** Their autonomous research scaffold is good at empirical work but makes choices that invalidate results and writes poorly; they ask why uplift is 2-3x rather than 10x. Commenters: experiment running faster, conceptual work and writing not. Practitioner report, no controlled data. IDs: R15.

**Terence Tao, Timothy Gowers, Thomas Bloom (mathematics).** Verification-first: AI proofs checked in Lean (Aristotle) or by fresh model instances plus expert review; Tao cautions that failures go unreported so success rates are unknowable; Gowers (secondary) says most celebrated results are counterexample searches. A claimed solution to Erdos #333 was wrong because the literature already had it. Bloom warns about 100-200 page AI papers no one reads. Primary Tao/Gowers texts not read. IDs: R19, R20, R21.

**Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar (evals).** Human-led error analysis: annotate at least 30 traces yourself, then let an agent find similar failures, human accepts or rejects, until saturation (page updated Sep 2026). Their trace study (secondary) found agents catch obvious in-trace contradictions but miss product judgment and flag good outputs. Do not let an LLM invent the taxonomy (secondary). Both sell training. IDs: R16, R17.

**Ethan Mollick (Wharton).** Empiricist-practitioner. Sep 2025: Sonnet 4.5 reproduced an economics paper from its archive, Stata to Python; he spot-checked and had GPT-5 Pro reproduce the reproduction; he stresses this is reproduction, not replication. Delegation loop: first pass, review, one or two corrections, otherwise do it yourself. No 2026 text found. IDs: R18.

**Jeremy Howard (Answer.AI / fast.ai).** Mar 2026 talk (secondary): AI coding resembles a slot machine and gives an illusion of control. Answer.AI sells Solveit (small steps, human edits AI output), so conflict of interest. The Aug 2026 fast.ai post is by Rachel Thomas. Primary talk not read. IDs: R27.

**Lorena Barba (reproducibility).** Sep 2026 paper: reproducibility practices (tests, commit history, repo structure, instructions, decision records) are context engineering for coding agents; the researcher remains responsible for verifying the artifacts. Position paper, no numbers. IDs: R23.

**Nicholas Carlini (Anthropic).** Mar 2026 "how to win a best paper": riskiest sub-problem first, about five started per one finished, kill ideas that can at best say "number goes up", sometimes work before reading the literature, reproduce results. Says nothing about AI use for research in that post. IDs: R22, R10.

**Shen and Tamkin (skill formation RCT, Jan 2026).** Randomised experiment on learning a new Python library (Trio): AI-assisted participants scored lower on a comprehension quiz; no significant speed gain; interaction style mattered. Authors are at Anthropic. IDs: R13.

Dropped as slogan-only or unreachable: Lemire (no 2026 text found), Steinhardt (no 2026 primary on agents), Schulman (only podcast summaries), Pavlo (only an aggregator report plus his Jan 2026 post, which does not discuss agents; see Not verified).

## Disagreements

1. **Can agents do research, or only engineering?** Berkeley ADRS and Anthropic's automated-researcher post show agents beating baselines and humans on tasks with a measurable score (R04, R11). Kapoor/Narayanan's shadow evaluations show failure when the task is an open question (R02, R03). They are mostly not in conflict: the split is verifiable metric vs open-ended. Fits this setting: the student's performance work is on the verifiable side for sweeps; the choice of what to measure is on the other side.
2. **Lab-reported speed-ups vs controlled evidence.** Anthropic staff report +20% to +50% and OpenAI reports record experiments per experimenter (R09, R12); METR's 2025 trial found a 19% slowdown and the skill-formation RCT found no significant average time gain (R14, R13); Redwood feels 2-3x against an implied 10x (R15). Labs have early models, self-selected users and compute growth (R09, R12). Fits: assume modest, uneven gains for you, and measure your own hours.
3. **Skip verification vs always verify citations.** Nanda often skips quote-checking because frontier models seem good (R01); Rao et al. measure fabricated URLs in 3-13% and worse rates for deep-research agents (R08). Dates differ (Sep 2025 vs Apr 2026), models differ; the measured study fits a student being judged on correctness.
4. **Understand everything vs agents run unsupervised.** Howard and Nanda (hand re-implementation, tutor use) vs Carlini's agent teams with no human in the loop and the OpenAI/Anthropic concurrency picture (R27, R01, R10, R12). Carlini himself voices unease (R10). Fits: Howard/Nanda for a junior who must defend every line (R13).
5. **Many starts vs few threads.** Carlini expects to start about five per one finished and says fail fast (R22); Willison is limited to one significant review at a time (R26); OpenAI describes concurrent sessions (R12). Reconciled by what a "start" costs: Carlini's starts are cheap probes of the riskiest piece.
6. **Kill early vs tolerate regressions.** Carlini: kill when results are merely positive (R22). Engram: conceptual leaps often score worse first, so do not prune by score (R24). Project level vs step level; both fit, with kill criteria written in advance and a rule that a regression with a stated mechanism is not a kill signal.
7. **Is the human or the model the main bottleneck?** Buck's commenters say judgment, taste and ideas (R15); Kapoor/Narayanan add resource awareness and instruction-following (R02). Both agree a human owns direction.
8. **Writing.** Redwood and commenters say agents write up poorly and papers pile up unwritten (R15); no source I found shows agents writing credible systems papers; Nanda says writing is your credential (R01). Fits: write the one-pager yourself.

## Disconfirming evidence and null results

- **"Delegate the literature review to deep-research tools."** Partly disconfirmed. Deep-research agents hallucinate URLs more than plain search-augmented models (R08). Agents' papers cited about half as many works as the human originals (R03). An agent-assisted Erdos claim failed because the solution was in a 1977 paper (R19). Nanda uses LLMs for literature but says to verify, then admits he often does not (R01). Support for delegating: automated-researcher teams use "librarian" agents and ablation suggests the literature review added headroom (20.7% vs 23.3% in one single-run ablation) (R11). Verdict: delegate retrieval and first summaries; do not delegate which papers matter or whether a citation supports a claim. I found no measurement of claim-support rates for 2026 tools beyond secondary snippets (R31, unverified).
- **"Idea tournaments or LLM councils pick good directions."** Null result: searched for studies of tournaments, councils and ranking of research ideas by LLMs (the searches that surfaced Nanda, shadow evaluations, ADRS, automated alignment researchers); none measured it. Indirect evidence against: agents rejected promising directions and added caveats rather than redesigning (R02, R03); human ideas fed to automated researchers did not improve them, while agents hill-climbing a given metric did well (R11); Nanda's multi-LLM cross-check is for hallucination (R01). I did not open the DeepMind co-scientist material, which the requester already knows.
- **"AI lets one junior run about nine projects at once."** No source supports it. Against: review bottleneck (R26, R25), 0-20% fully delegable (R09), no average speed-up in a learning RCT (R13), shadow-evaluation agents left budget unused and still produced reject-level work, so output volume is not value (R03), Mollick on low-value output (R18), Bloom on unread output (R19). The only supportive numbers are lab concurrency counts from a vendor without absolute figures (R12). Caveat: starting many cheap probes is supported (R22).
- **The draft lifecycle.** Missing: GPU smoke test before harness freeze (R22, R01); baseline reproduction check (R10, R04); prediction and kill criteria (R22, R16 for saturation-style stopping rules); supervisor checkpoint (R01). Misplaced: verification only after sweeps (R04: evaluator flaws propagate). Possibly unnecessary: lengthy archaeology of the literature before any probe (R22). Nothing I found argued the sequence itself is wrong. I did not find a source that studies "write the prediction before the run" or experiment registries for agents; searches for research-log and decision-log practice returned Barba and Nanda advice only (R01, R23).
- **METR.** The 2025 slowdown finding is now labelled historical by METR, and I could not confirm 2026 numbers (R14).
- **Reward hacking.** Anthropic found it in 2.4% of runs (R11) but the shadow evaluations found no significant evidence (R03). Do not assume absence.

## Transfer notes

| Practice | Transfers to a junior with 15 h/week, being evaluated? |
|---|---|
| Fixed-budget, single-metric agent loop (R05) | Yes, once the harness is validated; a 5-minute budget fits $90 of credit better than long runs; cost per run must be estimated first. |
| Evolutionary search frameworks (OpenEvolve etc., R04, R24) | Partly. Under $30 per problem is feasible, but they need a trustworthy simulator or cheap evaluator; hours-long GPU evaluations are explicitly out of scope (R04). |
| Fleet of parallel agents with monitors (R11, R10, R12) | No. Needs compute, many reviewer hours and monitoring infrastructure; the $18,000-$20,000 scale is 200x your credit. |
| Fresh-agent or cross-model review (R25, R18, R19) | Yes, cheap. |
| Hand re-implementation of key measurement (R01) | Yes and recommended; costs hours but is exactly what a hiring supervisor can probe. |
| Lean-style formal verification (R19) | Not directly; analogue is reference implementations, differential tests, known-answer checks. |
| Digest plus archive memory (R24) | Yes at small scale: a markdown digest and a results folder. |
| Research log and post-mortems (R01) | Yes; an hour per project is affordable only if projects are few. |
| Nanda's "pivot after 2 hours" and Carlini's start-5-finish-1 (R01, R22) | Needs adapting: with 15 h/week the unit is probably a session, not a project; time-box probes in hours, kill rules in writing. |
| Reporting honest negative results (R02, R03 show agents did this; R01) | Yes; a negative result with verified method is a credible signal to a supervisor. Evidence on supervisor perception: none found. |
| Using lab-grade tools and models (R09, R12) | Not guaranteed; lab staff may have stronger internal models (commenters, R15). |

## What better models change, and what they don't

Changed or likely changing (model-generation dependent): Karpathy's own stance moved from "agents unhelpful on this repo" (Oct 2025) to an agent-run research loop (Mar 2026) (R07, R05); METR calls its 2025 slowdown result historical (R14); Nanda told readers in Sep 2025 that his tool advice would go stale and gave Cursor over Claude Code (R01); ADRS authors expect improvement (R04); Kapoor/Narayanan expect verifiable engineering work to advance quickly (R02); OpenAI says delegation is moving to longer tasks (R12, vendor); Carlini's compiler reached the limit of Opus 4.6 (R10). Citation hallucination rates differ by model and by tool-use competence (R08). Hallucinated-citation numbers from 2025 tools should not be assumed valid for yours.

Not shown to change so far (as of the sources): agents under-using budget, ignoring limits, failing to backtrack and flagging their own work as weak but not acting on it (R02, R03); evaluator gaming (R11, R04); the need for a human to define what is worth measuring (R04, R11, R15); writing up (R15, R01); unread output (R19). Labs expect these gaps may close (R12 claims an automated researcher by March 2028; vendor, not evidence). Those who predict what stays human: Kapoor/Narayanan (judgment, resource management), ADRS (problem and evaluator design, audit), Anthropic staff (design, taste, tacit knowledge), Redwood commenters (idea quality, specifying what to measure) (R02, R04, R09, R15). A hedge: the skill-formation result and the "paradox of supervision" suggest your ability to supervise depends on practice you may skip (R13, R09).
